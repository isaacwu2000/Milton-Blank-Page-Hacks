## Integrated instructional rhythm

Review chat and artifact as one instructional exchange:

1. Chat briefly frames the idea and suggests an experiment when needed.
2. The learner manipulates the artifact and receives conceptual feedback.
3. The learner has time to digest the model.
4. Chat references the artifact's general relationship, supplies rigor, or asks one
   question.
5. Chat clarifies or advances the lesson based on the learner's answer.

Avoid repeated chat–visual–chat scanning during one manipulation. Instructions should be
complete enough that the learner can remain with the visual while it communicates.
Operating instructions may live in chat when that keeps the whiteboard focused. If a
later question assumes an action, the instruction to perform it must precede the
question.

Review the entire current Tutor session as the learning unit. A session may contain one
message now and grow into a longer exchange later. Judge whether its parts accumulate
into one intelligible learning experience rather than evaluating only the message nearest
the artifact.

## Learner calibration

Complexity is relative to the learner's demonstrated knowledge, course level, and recent
answers. A strong artifact can be too dense for a novice or too trivial for an advanced
learner.

Until durable learner memory exists, evaluate calibration from evidence inside the
current session. Do not require memory-driven continuity, inferred preferences, or
longitudinal direction that the product cannot yet provide.

When knowledge is uncertain, begin with a novice-legible model and make advanced depth
discoverable. Unknown terms, assumed prerequisite relationships, number of simultaneous
variables, and abstraction level should all match the learner.

The review should determine whether the surrounding chat establishes enough context for
the artifact's vocabulary and inference. Do not judge cognitive load without that context.

## Exploration and guidance

Free exploration and guided stages both have valid uses.

Free exploration works well for:

- forming a gist of a broad field or spatial system;
- discovering entities and relationships;
- building orientation before focused study;
- letting an advanced learner follow interest.

Its risks are aimless activity, unclear starting points, overlooked relationships, and
interaction without a takeaway.

Guided stages work well for:

- procedures and dependent sequences;
- focused mechanisms;
- proofs, derivations, and transformations;
- novice scaffolding;
- introducing one concept at a time.

Their risks are passive next-button behavior, fragmentation, hidden whole-system context,
and an imposed pace that prevents experimentation.

Choose intentionally. A useful hybrid often shows a simple whole, invites bounded
exploration, focuses one relationship, and later returns to the whole. This is one strong
strategy, especially for many historical and spatial subjects, but not a universal
default. A focused mathematical transformation may begin directly with the mechanism.

## Progressive disclosure

Progressive disclosure manages intrinsic cognitive load by introducing concepts in
phases. It should add meaningful specificity, not merely hide arbitrary content.

Broader exploration should present a simpler model. Focus may reveal greater detail.
Controls, annotations, and definitions relevant only to a selected entity or phase should
appear with that state and disappear when they lose their referent. Global controls may
persist when they change the whole knowledge model.

Preserve context needed to understand each reveal. Keep changing detail regions spatially
stable. Avoid turning progressive disclosure into a sequence of isolated cards.

## Questions and interaction state

Questions belong in Tutor chat, one at a time. Keeping questions out of the artifact
makes the visual reusable for later explanation and exploration.

Artifact manipulation is local and is not sent to chat. Tutor text must never claim to
have observed a control value, selected object, camera view, or completed action.

Chat may suggest an experiment. If a later question assumes that experiment, explicit
instructions to perform it must appear immediately before the question. Phrase later
discussion around the general relationship unless the learner reports their state.

Give every meaningful introduction of new material some evidence of understanding, but
do not turn the lesson into an endless chain of questions about edge cases. Once the
learner demonstrates the concept, provide direction and continue. More advanced material
may efficiently test prerequisite understanding while extending depth. Relate new
material to established knowledge when the relationship is instructionally meaningful.

## Rigor and intuition

Code artifacts primarily build intuition and gist. Chat supplies full formal reasoning,
detailed historical qualification, proof, derivation, and extended explanation.

The visual must still be formally honest. Geometry, transformation, scale, duration,
causality, and uncertainty cannot become false in the name of intuition. Chat should
extend the visual, not repair a misleading one.

## Terminology

Do not place required unknown terms in the artifact without learner-level help. Avoid
definitions that rely on equally unfamiliar terminology.

Use, in order:

1. a familiar plain-language label when precision is not lost;
2. a precise term paired with a short plain-language synonym or micro-definition;
3. a quiet information control beside the term when persistent definition text would
   overload the visual.

Definitions must work with pointer, touch, keyboard, and screen readers. If a term is
essential to the current state, its explanation must be available inside the artifact;
the learner should not have to search previous chat while interpreting the model.

## Misconceptions and uncertainty

Lead with the correct model. Expose a misconception when the learner begins to show it,
or when nearly all learners predictably hold it and comparison is necessary to establish
the correct understanding. The misconception earns space only when seeing why it fails
improves the learner's positive model; do not rehearse wrong ideas defensively.

Show uncertainty visually when it changes the knowledge model: approximate ranges,
contested overlaps, confidence bands, reconstructed surfaces, or competing
interpretations. Put longer qualification in chat. Use precise visual language rather
than vague blur or decorative uncertainty styling.

## Multiple representations

Use multiple representations when translating between them is educational: symbolic and
geometric, orbit and plan, chronological and proportional, microscopic and systemic.

Show them simultaneously only when direct comparison matters. Otherwise reveal them
progressively while preserving the connection. Avoid multiple views included merely to
appear comprehensive.

When multiple representations would overload the learner and translation is not the
artifact's direct lesson, use one representation for one message. Establish evidence that
the learner grasps it before introducing the next representation so their structures do
not blur together.

Related information should remain together. When physical adjacency is impossible, make
the relationship unmistakable through a leader, matching meaningful highlight, shared
symbol, synchronized motion, or another explicit connection. Do not rely on arbitrary
matching decoration.

## Qualifications and citations

Use this hierarchy:

- A qualification necessary to interpret the current visual stays visible beside the
  affected representation, such as `Not to scale`, `Approximate`, or `Reconstruction`.
- Short supporting qualifications appear as bullets in chat immediately after the
  artifact.
- A future collapsible qualifications component may hold long supplementary notes.
- Never collapse a caveat whose absence would make the visual misleading.

Keep full citations in chat. The artifact may show a concise source or dataset label when
provenance affects interpretation. A chart based on one survey, population, reporting
period, or dataset should not appear universally representative.

Verify central claims and implicit simplifications against authoritative sources.
Spot-check secondary labels. Preserve immutable source metadata with artifact versions
when the runtime supports it.
