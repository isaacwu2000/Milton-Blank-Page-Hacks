# Non-pedagogy quality

Evaluate the quality of the learning experience outside its teaching method. Apply this
section only after checking [block failures](block-failures.md). A block failure does not
erase these scores; continue diagnostically so the review remains useful.

Calculate points using the shared [scoring rules](scoring.md).

## Scoring method

Score each applicable Likert dimension from `1` to `5` using its complete rubric. Assign
the highest level whose description is substantially satisfied. A prominent defect named
at a lower level caps the score even when smaller details resemble a higher level.

- `1` represents actively poor execution.
- `3` represents competent, acceptable execution rather than excellence.
- `5` represents exemplary work with no meaningful weakness in the dimension.
- Use `N/A` only when the dimension genuinely does not apply.
- Use `Unknown` when the evidence is insufficient. Do not convert `Unknown` to a neutral
  score.

Do not award points twice for the same quality. For example, a malformed icon is recorded
as a cosmetic defect; its generic subject choice may also inform visual slop, but do not
describe the same optical-centering problem as two independent failures.

## 1. Research and source judgment

Evaluate whether the Tutor selects, combines, contextualizes, and qualifies the best
reasonably available evidence for the claims it makes. Source authority is claim-specific,
not a fixed domain-name hierarchy.

### Source selection order

Use the source type that best establishes the specific claim:

1. Direct primary evidence: official text, original dataset, statute, court decision,
   transcript, historical document, technical specification, or original research.
2. Evidence synthesis: systematic review, meta-analysis, scholarly consensus report, or
   major scientific assessment for a broad conclusion.
3. Strong academic research: relevant peer-reviewed work from credible researchers and
   journals, judged by methods, replication, relevance, recency, and how citations use it.
4. Authoritative institutions: government statistical agencies, universities,
   international organizations, and professional bodies acting within their expertise.
5. Reputable specialist analysis: transparent nonprofits, think tanks, research
   organizations, and specialist publications, with incentives and methodology considered.
6. Current expert material: preprints, engineering blogs, technical reports, conference
   material, and practitioner writing when the field moves faster than peer review.
7. High-quality journalism and reference sources for orientation, corroboration, and
   developing events.

A `.edu` or `.gov` domain is not sufficient evidence of quality. Citation count indicates
influence, not correctness; a paper may be cited because it is foundational, disputed, or
wrong. A primary source is strongest for what was said, enacted, measured, or observed,
while rigorous synthesis may be stronger for interpretation and generalization.

### Binary checks

- Every citation supports the exact nearby claim.
- No citation is fabricated, broken, or represented as stronger than it is.
- Central claims use the strongest reasonably available source type.
- Time-sensitive claims use sufficiently recent evidence.
- Preliminary, preprint, or non-peer-reviewed evidence is identified as such.
- Relevant methodological limits, institutional incentives, funding conflicts, and
  ideological biases are noted when they affect interpretation.
- Contested or high-stakes claims are triangulated when one source cannot establish them.
- Sources are included for evidentiary value rather than padded to simulate rigor.
- Citations are placed where the learner can tell which claim each source supports.

### Likert rubric: research judgment

#### 1 — Poor

Sources are absent where they are necessary, plainly unfit for the claims, outdated in a
time-sensitive domain, or chosen by prestige and domain name alone. The response treats a
single paper, institutional statement, or secondary report as conclusive without examining
what it establishes. Biases and material limitations are ignored.

#### 2 — Weak

Some credible sources appear, but selection is inconsistent. Stronger primary evidence or
synthesis was readily available; recency is uneven; citations establish only adjacent or
broader claims; or source limitations receive generic caveats. The research resembles a
source list more than a reasoned evidence base.

#### 3 — Competent

Sources are credible, relevant, and recent enough for the domain. Central claims are
supported and preliminary evidence is labeled. The Tutor notices obvious limitations and
biases, but may miss a stronger source, rely too heavily on one evidence type, or provide
only basic triangulation. The result is trustworthy but not unusually discerning.

#### 4 — Strong

Source type is matched intelligently to each claim. Primary material establishes direct
facts, synthesis supports broad conclusions, and recent expert material is used carefully
in fast-moving fields. Material biases and methodological limits are stated specifically.
Contested claims are triangulated without citation padding, and the evidence is integrated
into the explanation rather than appended decoratively.

#### 5 — Exemplary

The source set is both economical and exceptionally well judged. Each central claim uses
the strongest reasonably available evidence, with recency, methodology, replication,
institutional incentives, and disagreement handled in proportion to their importance. The
Tutor distinguishes direct evidence from interpretation, explains meaningful uncertainty
without drowning the learner in caveats, and makes the provenance of every consequential
claim immediately clear. No source is replaceable by an obviously stronger one.

## 2. Instruction fidelity and useful direction

Evaluate whether the Tutor fulfills the learner's actual request while gently improving
the learning direction when that would help. A pedagogical nudge is valuable only when it
is relevant and proportionate. It must not become an excuse to refuse a direct answer,
force a question, or substitute the Tutor's preferred lesson for the requested task.

### Binary checks

- The response addresses the learner's actual request rather than an adjacent, easier one.
- Explicit scope, format, artifact, tone, and interaction instructions are followed unless
  they conflict with accuracy, safety, or product constraints.
- Necessary limitations or deviations are explained directly.
- The response does not add unwanted artifacts, controls, digressions, tasks, or ceremony.
- Any pedagogical nudge preserves the requested outcome and does not require unnecessary
  interaction from the learner.

### Likert rubric: instruction fidelity and direction

#### 1 — Poor

The response misses or overrides the request, ignores important instructions, or delivers
a different task. It may use teaching as a pretext to withhold what the learner asked for,
or comply literally in a way that is unsafe or unusable.

#### 2 — Weak

The response partially fulfills the request but loses important scope, format, or intent.
It adds avoidable material, requires the learner to restate instructions, or introduces a
generic pedagogical detour unrelated to the immediate need.

#### 3 — Competent

The response fulfills the request accurately and directly, following the important
instructions without unnecessary additions. When a learning opportunity exists, it may
remain purely transactional or offer a generic next step. Nothing is wrong, but the
direction is not especially thoughtful.

#### 4 — Strong

The response fulfills the request precisely and makes the next useful learning move easier
when relevant. A concise framing sentence, experiment suggestion, connection, or optional
next step deepens the task without delaying the requested answer. When no pedagogical nudge
would help, the Tutor correctly stays direct.

#### 5 — Exemplary

The response feels exactly fitted to both the request and the learner's immediate purpose.
It preserves every important instruction, resolves ambiguity through context rather than
ceremony, and supplies only the additional direction that materially improves learning.
The nudge—when earned—naturally emerges from the requested work and leaves the learner
better oriented without feeling redirected, tested, or managed.

## 3. Cosmetic and production polish

Use the binaries to catch discrete defects, then score the three craft dimensions. A
learner-visible defect that affects operation or interpretation belongs under block
failures; these rubrics cover professional finish.

### Binary checks

- No typo, accidental misalignment, awkward wrap, stray border, inconsistent
  capitalization, or unexplained spacing anomaly is visible.
- Icons use Lucide or one convincingly Lucide-compatible family when Lucide lacks a
  sufficiently specific symbol.
- Icon stroke, size, optical centering, baseline, and selected-state treatment are
  consistent.
- Mathematical notation, CJK text, labels, and units use appropriate typography.
- No element looks provisional, unfinished, or accidentally inherited from a library
  default.
- Motion and state changes contain no visual jank, accidental delay, or rough transition.

### Likert rubric: layout, spacing, and hierarchy

#### 1 — Poor

The composition feels accidental. Spacing is inconsistent, alignment is visibly broken,
important content lacks prominence, and empty or crowded regions interfere with use.

#### 2 — Weak

The layout is usable but rough. Several gaps, alignments, widths, or hierarchy choices feel
arbitrary; the eye hesitates because unrelated elements compete or related elements drift
apart.

#### 3 — Competent

The layout is orderly and readable. Spacing generally follows a system and hierarchy is
understandable, though the composition may be timid, mechanically even, or less efficient
than the available surface permits.

#### 4 — Strong

Spacing, alignment, scale, and visual weight work together deliberately. Related content
groups naturally, the primary subject owns the appropriate area, and secondary material
remains quiet. The composition holds across required pane sizes with only negligible
refinements available.

#### 5 — Exemplary

The composition feels inevitable. Every region, alignment, proportion, and interval
supports immediate orientation and professional trust. Space is neither timid nor crowded;
the hierarchy remains exceptionally clear at every required size, and no visible adjustment
would materially improve rhythm or focus.

### Likert rubric: typography and notation

#### 1 — Poor

Type is difficult to read, inconsistent, malformed, or inappropriate to its role. Labels,
notation, line length, wrapping, or hierarchy actively reduce comprehension or trust.

#### 2 — Weak

Text is legible but visibly under-resolved. Sizing, weight, line height, wrapping,
capitalization, notation, or language-specific rendering contains repeated inconsistencies
or generic defaults.

#### 3 — Competent

Typography is clear, consistent, and appropriate. Mathematical and multilingual content
renders correctly. The hierarchy works, though details such as line length, optical size,
weight contrast, or label fit may remain ordinary rather than refined.

#### 4 — Strong

Typography creates a compact, confident hierarchy and handles prose, controls, notation,
units, and multilingual labels with care. Wrapping and line lengths feel authored, and type
supports the subject without calling attention to itself.

#### 5 — Exemplary

Every typographic decision is exceptionally resolved at the real pane size. Hierarchy,
rhythm, notation, language handling, and optical alignment form one precise system. The
type makes dense information feel effortless, with no ornamental or generic treatment.

### Likert rubric: icons, color, material, and motion

#### 1 — Poor

Symbols are crude or misleading; color is arbitrary or inaccessible; material and motion
feel broken, decorative, or disconnected from the subject.

#### 2 — Weak

The surface is functional but inconsistent. Icons vary in family or alignment, color roles
are vague, and material or motion looks like a default effect rather than a deliberate
choice.

#### 3 — Competent

Icons, colors, materials, and motion are coherent and usable. Semantic roles are mostly
clear and the surface fits LearnUnlock, but execution is conventional or contains minor
optical weaknesses.

#### 4 — Strong

Every visual device has a clear role. Icons are specific and polished, color carries
learnable meaning, material reinforces the subject, and motion makes state changes feel
direct and stable. The result is recognizably LearnUnlock without suppressing subject
identity.

#### 5 — Exemplary

Iconography, color, material, lighting, and motion form one immaculately crafted visual
language. Each choice is both beautiful and exact to its purpose; subject identity and
LearnUnlock interaction grammar reinforce one another, with no decorative or default
effect remaining.

## 4. Intentionality and anti-slop

Score visual and prose intentionality separately. Average the applicable scores for the
section result; do not add another holistic score that double-counts them.

Slop is not merely the presence of a banned style. It is evidence that the response was
assembled from category reflexes, generic templates, or language habits instead of being
authored for this learner, subject, and task.

### Visual signals to inspect

- Cards used as automatic layout units, especially nested cards.
- Headers, subheaders, pills, labels, and metadata that merely restate structure.
- Generic dashboard composition imposed on a teaching visual.
- Decorative gradients, glass, grids, giant radii, ghost shadows, or ambient motion.
- Repeated icon-heading-paragraph modules and library-default styling.
- Arbitrary color cycling or generic category palettes.
- Controls that visually dominate the represented subject.
- A first-order category cliché replaced by an equally predictable anti-cliché aesthetic.
- A polished surface with no subject-specific visual idea.

### Likert rubric: visual intentionality

#### 1 — Slop-dominated

The visual is unmistakably assembled from generic AI or template grammar. Multiple strong
tells dominate the composition, the subject could be swapped without redesign, and visual
effects substitute for a specific idea.

#### 2 — Clearly generic

The visual is functional but recognizably generated. Template layout, card scaffolding,
default controls, arbitrary decoration, or category clichés remain prominent. A few custom
details do not change the generic whole.

#### 3 — Competent

The obvious clichés are mostly absent and the composition has some subject-specific
decisions. A small number of generic patterns, safe defaults, or unearned visual devices
remain noticeable. The result is credible but not distinct.

#### 4 — Distinct

The visual is clearly designed for this subject, task, and pane. Familiar controls are
used without template dominance, decoration is restrained or meaningful, and the
composition has a coherent visual idea. Only subtle reflexes or missed opportunities
remain.

#### 5 — Fully authored

Every major visual choice feels deliberate, specific, and inseparable from the represented
idea. The design avoids both obvious clichés and fashionable anti-clichés, uses familiarity
where it improves trust, and expresses a unique subject-led composition without spectacle
or self-conscious originality.

### Prose signals to inspect

- Repeated em dashes as the default sentence joint.
- Repeated contrast templates such as `not X, but Y` or `more than X—it is Y`.
- Staged strawmen, meta-criticism, and ironic correction instead of direct claims.
- Excessive triads, mirrored clauses, or suspiciously symmetrical paragraphs.
- Inflated abstraction, fake profundity, nominalization, and unnecessary passive voice.
- Overly formal distance, generic lecture tone, or stock empathy and encouragement.
- Redundant summaries, headings that restate the next sentence, and excessive bullet or
  bold scaffolding.
- Vocabulary variation that sacrifices consistent terminology.

Natural prose may use em dashes, contrast, parallelism, and subordinate clauses. Judge the
repeated reflex, not the isolated construction. Sentence variation should follow meaning
rather than a quota.

### Likert rubric: prose intentionality

#### 1 — Slop-dominated

The prose is dominated by generic AI cadence, inflated abstraction, repetitive rhetorical
templates, or impersonal lecture voice. It sounds interchangeable across subjects and
obscures straightforward meaning.

#### 2 — Clearly generic

The prose communicates, but repeated em dashes, contrast formulas, triads, headings,
hedges, formal phrasing, or stock encouragement make the generation pattern conspicuous.
Some sentences are direct, but the overall voice remains synthetic.

#### 3 — Competent

The prose is clear, direct, and mostly natural. Sentence structure varies and terminology
is consistent, though occasional formulaic transitions, over-structured passages, or
generic phrases remain. The voice is trustworthy but not especially specific.

#### 4 — Distinct

The prose speaks directly and naturally, with varied sentence lengths and constructions
that follow the relationships being explained. Subordinate clauses clarify real
dependencies, rhetorical patterns are used sparingly, and the Tutor sounds interested in
this particular subject and learner.

#### 5 — Fully authored

The prose is concise, specific, human, and unmistakably fitted to the moment. Rhythm and
syntax vary without display; terminology stays exact; warmth appears without stock
sentiment; and every sentence advances the response. No reusable AI cadence, inflated
claim, redundant structure, or ornamental phrasing remains.

## 5. Perceived performance and professional trust

This dimension concerns the quality of working behavior. A delay, accessibility failure,
or broken state that impairs use belongs under block failures; score the remaining sense of
speed, stability, consistency, and readiness here.

### Likert rubric: interaction feel and professional trust

#### 1 — Poor

The experience feels unstable or unfinished even when it technically operates. Inputs lag,
state changes are confusing, controls feel improvised, or loading and motion repeatedly
interrupt the task.

#### 2 — Weak

The primary path works, but noticeable delay, rough transitions, inconsistent controls,
or uncertain feedback makes the learner pause. The surface feels like a prototype rather
than a dependable product.

#### 3 — Competent

The experience responds predictably and at an acceptable speed. Standard controls behave
as expected, loading and state changes are understandable, and nothing meaningfully erodes
trust. The result is solid but ordinary.

#### 4 — Strong

Interaction feels immediate, stable, and consistent across the session. Loading,
selection, focus, motion, and return states preserve flow; controls use a familiar
vocabulary; and the implementation feels ready for real learners under time pressure.

#### 5 — Exemplary

The interface disappears into the task. Every action receives immediate, proportionate
feedback; motion and loading preserve momentum; state transitions remain calm under
repeated use; and a learner familiar with the best modern tools would trust every detail
without hesitation. No interaction feels improvised or merely technically complete.

## 6. Responsive composition and natural subject geometry

Evaluate whether the composition remains faithful to the subject and instructional model
through the full range of required half-pane sizes. Responsive success means recomposing
available space without turning the subject into an unsuitable generic mobile layout.

### Binary checks

- The artifact is composed for its real portrait half-pane rather than an isolated
  full-width preview.
- The subject retains its natural orientation: commonly vertical timelines remain
  vertical, while inherently horizontal spatial systems remain horizontal.
- Text remains horizontal and readable; the layout never rotates labels to force fit.
- Resizing preserves the complete knowledge model before hiding or removing educational
  features.
- When uniform scaling would make labels or controls illegible, contextual information is
  repositioned or progressively disclosed without changing what the model means.
- Broad views reduce per-object detail, while focus states may recompose around one object
  and reveal greater relevant specificity.
- Space above, below, or beside a naturally horizontal subject is used for relevant
  controls or selected detail rather than stretching the subject into the container.
- Scrolling matches the subject: long timelines may use natural document scrolling,
  spatial worlds may pan and zoom, and bounded diagrams avoid generic nested scrolling.
- Desktop, laptop landscape, tablet landscape, and short landscape preserve hierarchy,
  readable text, usable controls, and the primary relationship.

### Likert rubric: responsive composition

#### 1 — Broken or distorted

The composition works only at one convenient size or materially distorts the subject to
fit. Essential content clips, disappears, rotates, becomes unreadable, or requires nested
scrolling that obscures the learning model. Responsive behavior changes what the visual
appears to mean.

#### 2 — Weak

The artifact remains technically usable at several sizes, but adaptations are generic or
awkward. Important regions shrink excessively, natural geometry is compromised, empty
space grows without purpose, or educational features disappear instead of being
recomposed.

#### 3 — Competent

The knowledge model, controls, and hierarchy remain understandable across required sizes.
The subject keeps an appropriate orientation and text remains readable. Some placements,
density changes, or use of secondary space feel ordinary or less efficient than they
could be.

#### 4 — Strong

The composition responds according to the subject. Scaling, reflow, focus, scrolling, and
progressive disclosure preserve truthful geometry and instructional hierarchy throughout
the required range. Each pane shape feels intentionally composed rather than tolerated.

#### 5 — Exemplary

The artifact feels native to every required half-pane while retaining one unmistakable
knowledge model. Natural geometry, density, labels, controls, and selected detail adapt
with exceptional economy; no size introduces avoidable translation work or sacrifices
educational value. Responsive behavior itself clarifies scope and focus.
